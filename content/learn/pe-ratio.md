## Basic: What is the P/E Ratio?

The **Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio** is one of the most widely used metrics in stock investing. It answers a simple question: *how much are investors willing to pay for every dollar a company earns?*

**Formula:** `P/E Ratio = Stock Price ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)`

For example, if a stock trades at $100 and its EPS is $5, the P/E ratio is **20x**. This means investors are paying $20 for every $1 of earnings.

**Think of it this way:** If you bought a small bakery for $100,000 and it earns $5,000 per year, you're paying a P/E of 20x — it would take 20 years of earnings to "pay back" your investment (ignoring growth).

## Intermediate: How to Interpret the P/E Ratio

P/E ratios vary significantly across sectors and market conditions:

| P/E Range | Interpretation |
|-----------|----------------|
| < 10x     | Potentially undervalued — or the market expects declining earnings |
| 10–20x    | Broadly "fair value" for mature, stable companies |
| 20–35x    | Growth premium — investors expect above-average earnings growth |
| > 35x     | High expectations — could be a growth stock or a valuation bubble |

**Important context:** The S&P 500 has historically traded at an average P/E of around **15–20x**. During bull markets, averages can exceed 25–30x.

**Types of P/E:**
- **Trailing P/E (TTM):** Uses the last 12 months of actual earnings — more reliable
- **Forward P/E:** Uses analyst estimates for the next 12 months — more speculative

### Limitations

- A **negative P/E** (loss-making company) is meaningless as a ratio
- P/E should always be compared to **sector peers** — tech companies typically have higher P/Es than utilities
- A low P/E can be a "value trap" if earnings are declining

## Advanced: P/E in Context of Market Cycles

The **Cyclically Adjusted P/E (CAPE or Shiller P/E)** uses inflation-adjusted earnings averaged over 10 years, smoothing out business cycle distortions. It was developed by economist Robert Shiller and is often used to assess broad market valuations.

**P/E Expansion vs. Contraction:**
- During low-interest-rate environments, investors accept higher P/Es (lower discount rates → higher present value of future earnings)
- When rates rise, P/Es compress — the same dollar of earnings is worth less when discounted at a higher rate
- This is why rising Fed Funds rates often lead to equity market sell-offs even when earnings hold steady

**The PEG Ratio** (P/E ÷ Growth Rate) adjusts for expected growth, providing a more nuanced view:
- PEG < 1.0: potentially undervalued relative to growth
- PEG > 2.0: potentially expensive relative to growth
