## Basic: What is Inflation?

**Inflation** is the gradual increase in the price of goods and services over time. If a grocery bag cost $50 last year and costs $53 today, that's 6% inflation on that basket of goods.

**The CPI (Consumer Price Index)** tracks price changes for a representative "basket" of ~80,000 items across 200 categories including food, housing, energy, and transportation.

**Think of it this way:** Inflation is like a slow tax on savings. If your bank account earns 1% interest but inflation is 4%, you're losing 3% of purchasing power each year.

## Intermediate: Causes of Inflation

There are two primary drivers of inflation:

**Demand-pull inflation:** "Too much money chasing too few goods." When consumers and businesses have more money to spend (from stimulus, wage growth, or easy credit), they bid up prices.

**Cost-push inflation:** When the cost of production rises (oil prices, wages, supply chain disruptions), businesses pass those costs to consumers.

### Core vs. Headline Inflation

- **Headline CPI:** Includes all items including volatile food and energy prices
- **Core CPI:** Excludes food and energy — used by the Fed as a more stable signal for monetary policy decisions

## Advanced: Inflation's Effect on Markets

**Stocks:** Moderate inflation (2–3%) is healthy for stocks as it reflects economic growth. High inflation hurts growth stocks most (future earnings are worth less when discounted at higher rates) while benefiting value stocks and commodity producers.

**Bonds:** Inflation is the enemy of bonds. As inflation rises, interest rates typically rise too — which causes existing bond prices to fall (price and yield move inversely).

**Real Assets:** Real estate, gold, and commodities often serve as inflation hedges since their prices tend to rise with general price levels.
